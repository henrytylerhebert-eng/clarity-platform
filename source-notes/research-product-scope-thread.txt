Three layers of recommendation logic:

Roommate compatibility — pairwise rules: acutely paranoid patient + confused wanderer who enters other people's rooms is a predictable patient-on-patient incident. Predatory history + vulnerable patient. Fall risk + bed far from the nursing station. The system flags conflicts and suggests swaps.
Adjacency and geography — who's next door and across the hall matters, not just who shares the room. High-stimulation patients near the dayroom vs. patients who decompensate with noise. Elopement risks away from exit-adjacent rooms.

Read it end to end. Verdict: adopt with three corrections and one structural change — this is a genuinely strong operational document, and its 10-step process maps almost one-to-one onto what we've already designed. Here's the vet:

What adopts cleanly. The 10-step process becomes the product's case pipeline: referral entry → legal status (our custody module, already built) → nurse-to-nurse clearance → "laying eyes" screening (the guided intake module) → psychiatric review → benefit verification → packet completion → bed assignment (Step 8 independently validates the milieu bedboard — gender/roommate safety, age category, acuity, ADA, isolation) → transport → handoff. The four roles become permission tiers with their own workspaces. And the KPI targets are a gift — referral-to-screening ≤1hr, intake-to-admission ≤4hrs, insurance verification ≤15min, ≥95% packet completeness, ≥85% conversion — because they slot straight into the live-clock house style. Every case gets SLA timers; the three scorecards (team monthly, individual quarterly, executive quarterly) are literally dashboard specs.

Correction 1 — the statutory deadline language needs tightening before it's encoded. Step 2 says "PEC expires after 72 hours; OPC must result in action within 12 hours of custody." The 72-hour figures in Title 28 attach to different things — exam-before-signing window, OPC validity, CEC-after-admission deadline — and the 12-hour exam-on-arrival rule is separate. In a paper SOP, loose phrasing is survivable; in software that enforces clocks, it isn't. This needs counsel to specify each clock's trigger event and duration precisely, then we encode exactly that.

Correction 2 — EMTALA demands a sequencing change. The SOP presents steps linearly, with benefit verification as Step 6. But its own risk section correctly notes that declining or delaying a psychiatric screening over insurance is an EMTALA violation. The software should run clinical (Steps 3–5) and financial (Step 6) as parallel lanes with a hard rule: financial status can never gate clinical progression. The business office's 15-minute SLA starts at referral entry, not after psych approval. That's an improvement software makes structurally that policy can only exhort.

Correction 3 — Step 5's psychiatrist approval needs a documented-decision artifact. The SOP says document the recommendation and name; in Clarity that becomes a ledger event with the psychiatrist's attestation, which is what survives a payer audit or a Title 28 challenge.

The structural insight: the SOP's own "what's missing" list is the software's feature list. Escalation matrix → automated SLA-breach routing (2-hour delay auto-notifies the Intake Director). Technology appendix → Clarity is the system of record. Forms repository → versioned form packs. Command-center script and color-coded bed status → the live dashboard. Payer-specific quirks → the criteria packs. Surge protocol → a declarable high-volume mode that relaxes routing and activates overflow queues. Every gap the document identifies in itself is something policy can't fix but product can.
